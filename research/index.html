Job Market Paper

Capacity Utilization in Production Networks

PDF

This paper quantifies how capacity constraints shape the propagation of sectoral shocks through the production network and their long-run cost. I build a dynamic production network model in which output is capped by a capacity proportional to capital, solve it as a graphon mean-field game and calibrate it to 63 US industries. Capacity constraints lower GDP per unit of capital by about 10 per cent, mostly through wedges paid along every production chain, and dampen the propagation of sectoral shocks to about three quarters of the unconstrained response, most for upstream and tight sectors.

Labor Market Signals: The Role of Large Language Models

with Giuseppe Musillo, Till Wicker and Niccolò Zaccaria · Forthcoming, Journal of Labor Economics · Journal · PDF (accepted manuscript)

Large Language Models (LLMs) are transforming the labor market, including hiring decisions. This paper examines their impact on the signals job-seekers send to potential employers through two field experiments focusing on cover letters. We find that LLMs enhance signal quality, especially benefiting lower-quality applicants, yet these gains do not boost interview invitations because they are concentrated in standardized, less influential sections of the cover letter. When recruiters learn of LLM usage, they place greater value on high-quality, human-crafted letters. Hence, LLMs reduce the informativeness of signals, potentially increasing inefficiencies in labor market matching as estimated by a calibrated structural model.

Heterogeneous Beliefs, Learning and Multiplicity in Directed Technical Change

CentER Discussion Paper 2024-020 · CentER Best Graduate Student Paper Prize 2024 PDF · Tilburg research portal

The adoption of new technologies occurs at vastly different rates, and is often accompanied by heterogeneous beliefs about their future productivity. Due to strategic complementarities in innovation, transitions to new substitute technologies also manifest as coordination problems with their inherent multiplicity. This paper explores the pivotal role of imperfect information and beliefs in technological change, emphasising the reciprocal relationship between innovation pace and beliefs. A tractable continuous-time two-sector directed technical change model incorporating rational learning about the research productivity of new inventions reveals that belief heterogeneity leads to research misallocation, but it can also significantly influence the transition trajectory and equilibrium selection. A calibration to dirty versus clean technologies shows that with enough optimism about new (clean) technologies, the economy can transition away from otherwise locked-in old (dirty) technologies.

The Capacity–Inventory Tradeoff

Firms can buffer against unexpectedly high demand with spare capacity or with inventories. Over the past four decades, both the inventory-to-sales ratio and the rate of capacity utilization in the US have trended down, which suggests that firms have shifted from inventories to spare capacity. I build a model in which firms choose how much of each buffer to hold, and use it to quantify the causes and consequences of this shift. Two candidate causes are the falling relative price of capital goods, which makes idle capacity cheaper to hold, and the growth of product variety, which makes inventories a less useful buffer. The shift matters because inventories let firms smooth production, while spare capacity lets them smooth prices, so it changes how demand shocks, and potentially monetary policy, pass through to output and prices.

AI at the Gate: Platform Answers and the Supply of News

with Giuseppe Musillo, Till Wicker and Niccolò Zaccaria · Draft available on request

How does generative AI affect competition and production in markets for information? We study this question through Google's AI Overviews, which provide AI-generated answers above search results. We combine weekly internet traffic data of major US and UK news outlets with the launch of AI Overviews to build a novel outlet-level measure of exposure to AI, the share of an outlet's articles containing no original reporting, and provide a causal estimate of the effect of generative AI on the market for information. We show that a one-standard-deviation increase in replaceability is associated with a 10.8% larger decline in visits after launch and an 8.7% decline in monthly unique visitors. We develop a general-equilibrium model in which AI platforms use news outlets' content to generate answers while competing with them for readers. We show that compensating outlets for lost traffic would mostly benefit producers of replaceable content, whose decline is largely efficient: generated answers supply the same information at a lower cost in readers' time. Instead, payments for the use of original reporting sustain the production of information that AI cannot reproduce, while preserving the time savings from AI-generated answers.

On the Dynamics of the Firm Size Distribution

Draft available on request

The right tail of the US firm size distribution has thickened steadily since the late 1970s. This paper asks how much of this shift reflects changes in the parameters governing firm dynamics, as opposed to slow convergence to an unchanged steady state. I study a random growth model of firm entry, exit and growth in the style of Luttmer (2007) and, through a spectral decomposition of its Fokker–Planck operator, derive exact solutions for the transition of the firm size density. The speed of convergence is a closed-form sufficient statistic: it increases in growth volatility, entry and exit, decreases in mean growth, is unchanged for Pareto-shaped initial distributions, and is higher when firm size is bounded above. Volatility is the main stabilising force, smoothing local deviations from the steady state, which explains why multimodal size distributions are short-lived. Calibrated to US Census data, the model with constant parameters generates about 70 per cent of the observed speed of decline in the tail index through transition dynamics alone; a steady-state interpretation would instead require a counterfactual rise in firm growth volatility.
